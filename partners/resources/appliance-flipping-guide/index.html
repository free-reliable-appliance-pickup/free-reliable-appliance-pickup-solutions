How to Flip Used Appliances: From Pickup to Resale

A practical field guide for appliance buyers, resellers and pickup partners. The goal is not to take every machine. The goal is to identify the units you can evaluate, move, test and resell responsibly before time and repair costs erase the margin.

Start With the Exit Plan, Not the Appliance

A free or inexpensive appliance can still be a bad pickup. Before loading anything, decide what the likely next step is: direct resale, cleaning and retesting, a known repair, parts recovery or responsible recycling. If you cannot describe a realistic exit path, the appliance may become storage instead of inventory.

Demand

Ask whether this type, size and finish actually sells in your area. Common, clean, standard-size machines are often easier to place than unusual or oversized units.

Condition

Separate verified working, partly working, repair-needed, unknown and scrap units. “Looks clean” is not the same as “tested working.”

Access

Stairs, tight doors, long carries, gates and difficult parking change the real cost of a pickup before the appliance reaches your vehicle.

Transport

Know whether the machine can be moved upright, secured correctly and unloaded safely with the equipment and help you actually have.

The Simple Margin Test

Estimate the full job before you commit. A useful worksheet is:

Expected resale price − acquisition cost − fuel/transport − parts − paid labor − cleaning supplies − marketplace/payment fees − delivery cost = estimated margin.

Add a buffer for surprises. If the deal only works when every assumption is perfect, it is probably not a strong pickup. Time matters too: two hours spent recovering a low-demand machine can block a better pickup later in the day.

What to Test Before You Load

Whenever the homeowner can safely demonstrate the machine, get the best condition information you can before transport. If a machine cannot be tested, label it unknown rather than working.

Washer

Power, fill, agitation or wash action, drain, spin, door/lid lock, leaks and unusual noise.

Dryer

Power, tumble, heat, control operation, abnormal noise and whether it is gas or electric.

Refrigerator

Power, cooling history, freezer temperature, compressor/fan sound, ice buildup, door seals and obvious water leaks. Ask whether it was recently unplugged.

Range or Oven

Power or gas type, surface elements/burners, oven heat, controls, door condition and obvious damage. Do not improvise unsafe utility testing.

Freezer

Power, cooling history, lid/door seal, frost condition, interior odor and signs of water or rust damage.

Dishwasher

Because installed dishwashers can involve plumbing, electrical connections and cabinet access, confirm removal status and access before treating one like a normal curbside pickup.

Safety first: do not bypass electrical, gas, refrigeration or safety systems to prove a machine works. If safe testing is not possible, record the condition as unknown and price the risk accordingly.

Photos That Save Wasted Trips

Ask for photos before driving whenever possible. One good photo can answer questions that a long text conversation cannot.

Full front view

Shows finish, controls, missing parts and overall cosmetic condition.

Inside/interior

Useful for refrigerators, freezers, ovens and washers when cleanliness or missing shelves/racks matter.

Model label

Helps identify size, age range, configuration and parts before you commit.

Damage close-up

Dents, rust, cracked handles, torn seals, broken knobs and control-panel damage should be visible before pickup.

Removal path

Stairs, doorway width, narrow halls, gates and loading areas can be more important than the appliance itself.

Use a consistent condition system. It makes better pickup decisions and makes customer communication more accurate.

Transport Is Part of the Deal

A profitable appliance can become an expensive loss if it is damaged during removal. Use appropriate appliance-moving equipment, secure the load, protect finished surfaces and plan the path before lifting. Refrigeration equipment may have manufacturer-specific transport and restart considerations, so follow the appliance manufacturer's instructions when available.

Do not promise inside removal, stair carries, disconnection or utility work until you know the access and have the right people and equipment. A pickup partner should be able to decline an unsafe removal.

Clean, Retest, Document

After transport, test again before advertising a machine as working. Document what you actually verified. Clean the appliance thoroughly enough that a buyer can inspect it without hidden residue or odor, and photograph the exact unit you are selling.

A strong listing describes the machine accurately: model when known, dimensions when relevant, tested functions, known defects, cosmetic condition, pickup/delivery terms and what is not included. Avoid claiming “like new” or “fully working” when the test does not support it.

Build Inventory From Better Sources

One-off marketplace pickups can work, but repeatable sources are more valuable because they reduce the time spent searching. Property managers, apartment turns, senior communities, installers, remodelers, retailers, hospitality operators and other recurring replacement sources can generate multiple opportunities from one relationship.

A Practical Pickup Decision Checklist

Before accepting a unit, answer these questions:

  1. What exact appliance is it, and what is the model or approximate size?
  2. What functions were actually tested?
  3. What defect or cosmetic damage is already known?
  4. Can I see clear photos of the appliance and removal path?
  5. What floor is it on, and are there stairs, elevators, gates or long carries?
  6. How far is the pickup from my current route?
  7. What is my realistic resale price—not the highest asking price I can find?
  8. What parts, labor, cleaning, transport and delivery costs are likely?
  9. If the repair fails, do I still have a responsible parts or recycling exit?
  10. Does this pickup make sense compared with the next opportunity?

What New Flippers Usually Get Wrong

Taking everything

More inventory is not automatically more profit. Selectivity keeps storage, disposal and repair backlog under control.

Pricing from asking prices

Listings show what sellers want, not always what buyers actually pay. Use conservative estimates until you know your local market.

Skipping the second test

A machine that worked before transport can develop a problem or reveal one later. Retest before advertising.

Ignoring access

A “free” machine upstairs with a difficult carry can cost more in time and risk than a paid unit sitting in a garage.

Hiding defects

Accurate descriptions protect your reputation and reduce wasted messages, returns and disputes.

No exit route

Know what you will do if a repair is uneconomical before you add the unit to your storage area.
